01
The problem

A million free ellipsoids

3D Gaussian Splatting renders beautifully, in real time. It also has no idea where the surface is.

≈ 5 min
Intuition

3DGS: a scene is a cloud of blobs

Every Gaussian owns its position, shape, colour and opacity. An optimiser nudges each one, independently, until the rendered images match the photographs.

This is flatland — a 2‑D slice of the idea. The cameras see 1‑D strips of pixels, and the optimiser on the right is real: it is running now, in your browser.

Intuition

Now look from somewhere new

The training strips match almost perfectly. But move to a camera the optimiser never saw and the picture breaks: many Gaussians sit off the surface entirely. These are floaters.

Drag the held‑out camera
Mechanism

Depth along a ray is a free choice

Down to two views, about half of the floaters are seen by one camera only. Slide one along its ray and that camera’s strip barely changes — the figure measures it. The loss cannot tell, and nothing else ties a Gaussian to a surface.

A free Gaussian has every degree of freedom, including the ones we don’t want.

Mechanism

The usual repair: constrain the cloud

  • Prune and densify by accumulated gradient statistics — heuristics, tuned by ablation.
  • Flatten and align — SuGaR, 2DGS, PGSR: push Gaussians toward a surface with regularisers.
  • Bind to a mesh — MeshGS, GaussianMesh: anchor Gaussians to triangles.

All of them start from unstructured primitives and ask them to resemble a surface afterwards.

Intuition

Our move: invert the question

Everyone elseGiven optimised Gaussians, how do we extract a surface?
Knot‑a‑SurfaceGiven a smooth surface, how do we derive the Gaussians?

Same photos, same loss. But now the only unknowns are the amber control points of one curve — 161 numbers instead of 576. The Gaussians ride on it.

Mechanism

Same loss. Same views. Same steps.

The free cloud wins the training photos by a wide margin — and loses the geometry. The bound model lands at roughly half the surface error, with a fraction of the off‑surface Gaussians, on under a third of the parameters.

A bound Gaussian cannot leave its surface. It can only be wrong if the whole curve is wrong, and every view constrains the curve at once.

Honest footnote: slide the views up and the free cloud’s extra capacity wins on photo quality again. The geometry gap stays.

02
Splines from scratch

What is a spline?

Before surfaces: one curve, and the three ideas that make it the right thing to optimise.

≈ 6 min
Intuition

A spline was a strip of wood

Ship draughtsmen bent a thin flexible strip and pinned it in place with lead weights. The strip settles into the smoothest curve the pins allow.

We keep the idea and drop the wood: the pins become control points, the strip becomes a curve.

Intuition

Control points steer. The curve follows.

The curve doesn’t pass through its control points — it is pulled toward them. Seven points here describe a curve with no resolution limit: zoom in forever, it stays smooth.

Drag any amber point
Intuition

Every point is a weighted average

At parameter , the curve is a blend of the nearest control points. The blending weights are the basis functions in the plot below — they always sum to one.

Mechanism

Local support: edits stay local

Each basis function is non‑zero over only spans. Move one control point and only the bright part of the curve moves; the dashed ghost shows where it was.

For an optimiser this is gold: a gradient step in one place cannot wreck another.

Mechanism

Degree buys smoothness

Degree 1 is a polyline. Degree 2 has continuous tangents. Degree 3 has continuous curvature — across every joint.

Smooth tangents mean smooth normals, and smooth normals are exactly what a splat needs to orient itself.

Mechanism

Knots: where the pieces join

The knot vector cuts the parameter line into spans. Slide a knot and resolution moves along the curve; crowd them and detail concentrates.

Non‑uniform knots put capacity where the shape needs it. That is the knot in Knot‑a‑Surface.

Drag the amber triangles
Math

Rational weights: exact circles

Give every control point a weight and divide. Conics — circles, cylinders, spheres — become exact, not approximated. Set all weights to 1 and watch the circle fail.

Non‑Uniform · Rational · B‑Spline = NURBS, the surface format of every CAD system.

Math

Cox–de Boor: built from boxes

Degree 0 is a row of boxes, one per knot span. Each higher degree blends two neighbours from the row above. The highlighted family is everything one cubic depends on.

03
The core idea

Calculus makes the splat

A Gaussian needs a position, an orientation and a size. The surface already knows all three.

≈ 8 min
Intuition

Two parameters make a surface

Blend along , then along . A net of 36 control points sweeps out a smooth sheet — a tensor product of the curve you just met.

Intuition

Pick a point (u, v)

Position is just evaluation. The Gaussian is on the surface because it is a point of the surface.

Drag the cross in the (u, v) chart
Mechanism

Differentiate

The surface is a polynomial in and , so its partial derivatives are closed‑form. They give two tangent vectors at every point — no finite differences, no neighbours, no learning.

Mechanism

Orientation is the tangent frame

Cross the tangents for the normal. Stack the three unit vectors and you have the Gaussian’s rotation.

Mechanism

Size is the distance to the next sample

Stretch each axis by how far the surface travels across one sampling cell, and keep it paper‑thin along the normal. A flat Gaussian on the surface: a planoid.

Intuition

Nothing here was learned

Move the surface and the Gaussian is recomputed: new position, new tilt, new size. Rotation and scale are not parameters any more — they are consequences.

Drag an amber control point
Intuition

Sample the chart, tile the surface

Lay a grid over the (u, v) square and drop one planoid per sample. Because each one is sized to its own cell, the tiling has no holes — at any density.

Intuition

The resolution knob

From 36 Gaussians to 22,500 and back. The learnable parameters do not move: 576, the whole time.

A trained 3DGS model cannot do this — its Gaussian count is baked in. Here, detail on screen is a rendering decision: re‑evaluate, don’t retrain.

Take the samples slider
Math

Appearance rides the same basis

Colour (spherical‑harmonic coefficients) and opacity live on the control points too — control features — and are interpolated by the same basis functions as position.

04
Lightweight by construction

The parameter ledger

What does each representation actually have to store — and to learn?

≈ 3 min
Intuition

A free Gaussian is 59 numbers

Position, scale, rotation, opacity, and 48 spherical‑harmonic coefficients for view‑dependent colour. Every Gaussian carries its own copy, and every one is optimised.

Intuition

Ours: 16 per control point. Zero per Gaussian.

Geometry is carried by the surface, so position, rotation and scale cost nothing. And because that geometry is right, low‑order colour is enough: SH degree 1 instead of 3.

Mechanism

At the scale of a scene

One million Gaussians on screen, either way. One model optimises 59 million numbers; the other optimises its control net.

Exact arithmetic over the sliders, not a benchmark. The green band marks the 10–50× the paper reports.

Move the sliders
Intuition

Want more detail on screen?

One ledger grows with every Gaussian you add. The other does not move — the knob from the last chapter costs nothing to store.

05
Adaptive refinement

Two knobs, not one

3DGS adds detail one way: clone or split a Gaussian. We have two layers, and they don’t interfere.

≈ 6 min
Intuition

Start coarse

A 5 × 5 control net gets the rolling ground roughly right and misses the crater: 6.3 % error overall, 45 % at worst. Where is no mystery — error is measured per knot span, along on top, along on the right.

Mechanism

Layer A — insert a knot

Add a knot in the worst span and a new row of control points appears there. At the instant of insertion the surface does not change — it only gains freedom. Then the optimiser spends it: one knot is a small step, 6.3 → 6.0 %.

Compare with 3DGS densification, which decides by accumulated view‑space gradients and spawns new, independent primitives.

Math

Why the surface stays put

Boehm’s knot insertion replaces the control points near the new knot by convex combinations of the old ones. Same curve, one more handle.

Intuition

Repeat where it hurts

Eighteen insertions later: 195 control points, 0.9 % error. Knots crowd over the crater; the flat ground stays cheap. Every new parameter is a control point on the surface, so refinement can never create a floater.

Mechanism

…and remove where it doesn’t

The reverse move prunes knots the fit no longer needs: take one out, re‑fit, keep the change only if the error holds. Three go; 156 control points remain at 0.92 %.

Mechanism

Same parameters, spent better

The adaptive net: 0.92 % error. A uniform net of the same size: 4.4 %. The best uniform net we could find near that size still sits at 3.1 %.

All three are fitted live by the same optimiser against the same target.

Intuition

Layer B — sampling density

Now change how many Gaussians are drawn. The surface, its error and its parameter count do not move.

Capacity lives in the knots. Rendering resolution lives in the samples. Two decisions, cleanly separated.

06
Scaling to scenes · full power

A manifold of splines

One patch is a bent rectangle. A real scene needs an atlas of them.

≈ 7 min
Intuition

A barn, entirely derived

Every Gaussian you see was computed from a spline chart a moment ago. Ground, walls, roof, silo, treeline: 11,188 control points in all, and nothing else is stored.

A procedural stand‑in modelled after a Tanks and Temples capture — not the dataset. The white frusta are its handheld‑orbit camera rig.

Drag to orbit
Intuition

An atlas of charts

The scene is split into coherent surface pieces, and each piece gets its own (u, v) domain. Together they cover the scene: a spline manifold.

Charts can close on themselves — the silo wraps around once; the treeline is one long ribbon around the horizon.

Mechanism

Each chart carries its own net

Shape and appearance both live on the amber points. A flat wall needs almost nothing for its geometry and a great deal for its planks and windows; the ground is the opposite. Parameters are paid where there is something to describe.

Intuition

A fixed budget follows the camera

The Gaussian count never changes. As the camera moves, samples flow across the (u, v) domains toward what is visible and facing the lens; a small floor keeps everything else covered.

Watch the atlas, bottom left: brighter tiles are holding more Gaussians. Sparsely described charts — ground, treeline — get a smaller share: there is no point sampling finer than a surface’s own detail.

Intuition

Level of detail, without retraining

The same budget, two ways. Off: spread by surface area, and the close‑up is built from coarse scales. On: spent where the camera looks.

Nothing was optimised between the two. Only the sampling changed.

Intuition

Same method, another capture

A pickup on a street. The body is one closed chart lofted from tail to nose; each wheel is a surface of revolution; the street and the wall behind are two more charts.
