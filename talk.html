01
The problem

A million free ellipsoids

3D Gaussian Splatting renders beautifully, in real time. It also has no idea where the surface is.

≈ 5 min
Intuition

3DGS: a scene is a cloud of blobs

Every Gaussian owns its position, shape, colour and opacity. An optimiser nudges each one, independently, until the rendered images match the photographs.

This is flatland — a 2‑D slice of the idea. The cameras see 1‑D strips of pixels, and the optimiser on the right is real: it is running now, in your browser.

Intuition

Now look from somewhere new

The training strips match almost perfectly. But move to a camera the optimiser never saw and the picture breaks: many Gaussians sit off the surface entirely. These are floaters.

Drag the held‑out camera
Mechanism

Depth along a ray is a free choice

Down to two views, about half of the floaters are seen by one camera only. Slide one along its ray and that camera’s strip barely changes — the figure measures it. The loss cannot tell, and nothing else ties a Gaussian to a surface.

A free Gaussian has every degree of freedom, including the ones we don’t want.

Real captures have many more views. The argument is about the under‑observed part of a scene: grazing angles, thin structures, background.

Mechanism

The usual repair: constrain the cloud

  • Prune and densify by accumulated gradient statistics — heuristics, tuned by ablation.
  • Flatten and align — SuGaR, 2DGS, PGSR: push Gaussians toward a surface with regularisers.
  • Bind to a mesh — MeshGS, GaussianMesh: anchor Gaussians to triangles.

The first two push a free cloud to resemble a surface; the third ties Gaussians to a fixed, faceted mesh. None makes a smooth, re‑samplable surface the thing being optimised.

Intuition

Our move: invert the question

Everyone elseGiven optimised Gaussians, how do we extract a surface?
Knot‑a‑SurfaceGiven a smooth surface, how do we derive the Gaussians?

Same photos, same loss. But now the only unknowns are the orange control points of one curve — 161 numbers instead of 576. The Gaussians ride on it.

Mechanism

Same loss. Same views. Same steps.

The free cloud wins the training photos by a wide margin — and loses the geometry. The bound model lands at roughly half the surface error, with a fraction of the off‑surface Gaussians, on under a third of the parameters.

A bound Gaussian cannot leave its surface. It can only be wrong if the whole curve is wrong, and every view constrains the curve at once.

Honest footnotes: this toy’s true curve is smooth, the best case for a spline — the constraint trades variance for bias. And slide the views up: the free cloud’s extra capacity wins on photo quality again. The geometry gap stays.

Intuition

Only the curve stays

Take away the cameras, the photos and the Gaussians, and one curve is left: the thing the bound model optimises. Next: what a curve like this is made of.

The curve that stays is this toy’s true curve, written as a 14‑point cubic spline; the bound model’s curve, wherever its training stands, eases onto it (scripted).

02
Splines from scratch

What is a spline?

Before surfaces: this one curve, and the three ideas that make it the right thing to optimise.

≈ 3½ min
Intuition

A spline was a strip of wood

Ship draughtsmen bent a thin flexible strip and pinned it in place with lead weights. The strip settles into the smoothest curve the pins allow.

We keep the idea and drop the wood: the pins become control points, the strip becomes a curve.

Intuition

Control points steer. The curve follows.

The curve doesn’t pass through its control points — it is pulled toward them. Fourteen points here describe a curve with no resolution limit: zoom in forever, it stays smooth.

Drag any orange point
Intuition

Every point is a weighted average

At parameter , the curve is a blend of the nearest control points. The blending weights are the basis functions in the plot below — they always sum to one.

Mechanism

Local support: edits stay local

Each basis function is non‑zero over only spans. Move one control point and only the bright part of the curve moves; the dashed ghost shows where it was.

For an optimiser this is gold: a gradient step in one place cannot wreck another.

Mechanism

Degree and knots: the two dials

Degree buys smoothness: a cubic has continuous curvature, so its normals turn smoothly, and that is what a splat needs to orient itself. Knots are where the polynomial pieces join. Slide one and resolution moves along the curve; crowd them and detail concentrates.

Non‑uniform knots put capacity where the shape needs it. That is the knot in Knot‑a‑Surface.

Add a weight per control point and conics become exact: that is the R in NURBS. In everything that follows the weights stay at 1: plain B‑splines.

The core idea

Calculus makes the splat

A Gaussian needs a position, an orientation and a size. The surface already knows all three.

≈ 8 min
Intuition

Two parameters make a surface

Blend along , then along . A net of 36 control points sweeps out a smooth sheet — a tensor product of the curve you just met.

Here the 36 heights are fitted to a terrain by least squares: conjugate gradients pull a flat net onto it in 25 steps, from 14 % to 5.7 % RMS error of the relief. Six points a side hold the rolling ground and blur the crater, where the red misses gather. Chapter 05 starts from that crater.

Intuition

Pick a point (u, v)

Position is just evaluation. The Gaussian is on the surface because it is a point of the surface.

Drag the cross in the (u, v) chart
Mechanism

Differentiate

The surface is a polynomial in and , so its partial derivatives are closed‑form. They give two tangent vectors at every point — no finite differences, no neighbours, no learning.

They are two different directions, and together they span the tangent plane. The inset shows that plane face‑on, with the true angle between them.

Mechanism

Orientation is the tangent frame

Cross the tangents for the normal. Stack the three unit vectors and you have the Gaussian’s rotation.

The two tangents are rarely perpendicular, so the second axis is Sv with its Su part removed.

Mechanism

Size is the distance to the next sample

Stretch each axis by how far the surface travels across one sampling cell, and keep it paper‑thin along the normal. A flat Gaussian on the surface: a planoid. Each thin ring is one Gaussian’s 1σ edge.

That is what the scaling buys. Give all 81 Gaussians one size, the mean cell, and the stretched rim opens up: about 5.5 % of the surface is left uncovered (red) while 17 % is piled three deep. Each sized to its own cell: nothing uncovered, 3.6 % three deep.

σ is three quarters of the cell here, so neighbours overlap.

Switch one size / own cell
Math

The neighbours agree

The size is a statement about neighbours. Measured from the cell’s four neighbours in the (u, v) lattice, half the chord between opposite ones, it agrees with the derivative to about 8 % at 9² samples and 0.4 % at 40². The gap falls as 1/n²; the derivative needs no neighbours at all.

Fitting all eight neighbours gives the same area plus the shear between Su and Sv, which this two‑length formula leaves out.

Switch 4 / 8 neighbours, then move the samples slider
Intuition

Nothing here was learned

Move the surface and the Gaussian is recomputed: new position, new tilt, new size. Rotation and scale are not parameters any more — they are consequences.

An oriented ellipse sized to its sample is classic surfel splatting. What is new is that the surface underneath is the thing learned from photographs.

Drag an orange control point
Intuition

Sample the chart, tile the surface

Lay a grid over the (u, v) square and drop one planoid per sample. Sized to its own cell, each Gaussian reaches its neighbours: no part of the surface is left uncovered at any sample count we tried, from 6² to 160².

One size for all, with the same total area, leaves about 4–8 % of the surface uncovered (red) along the stretched rim, and piles up in the middle: at 14², 18 % of the surface three deep, against 4 % sized to the cells.

Uncovered: the share of the surface’s area that no 1σ outline encloses, tested on a fine (u, v) grid. Each planoid is flat, so on a curved surface it is exact only at its centre; the gap shrinks as the samples get denser.

Mechanism

A mesh from a sliding window

The samples sit on a lattice, so each one already knows its neighbours. A 2 × 2 window slides over the 12 × 12 lattice and stitches two triangles at every stop: 121 stops, 242 triangles, nothing searched. The Gaussians it has passed are retired.

Chapter 07 runs the same window over the scanned owl.

Intuition

The resolution knob

From 64 Gaussians to 1,048,576, four times as many at every step. The 576 stored numbers do not move: every Gaussian is evaluated from them on demand, as densely as we ask.

What stops the sweep is drawing, and the frame time is measured on screen: it turns back when a frame takes longer than 50 ms, or at a million, the largest buffer this page sets aside. A trained 3DGS model cannot do this — its Gaussian count is baked in. Here, how finely the surface is drawn is a rendering decision: re‑evaluate, don’t retrain.

On a machine whose first frame is already slow, the limit is three times that frame. More samples draw the spline more faithfully; they cannot add detail the control net does not hold — that takes a knot.

Take the Gaussians slider
Intuition

A fixed budget, spent where a camera looks

The samples need not be even. Keep 1,024 Gaussians and pull the lattice, like a net of springs, towards what a second camera sees, weighted by how large each piece of surface appears in its picture.

About two thirds of the Gaussians end up in its view, which covers 14–32 % of the surface as it flies. There a cell is 1.4–1.9 % of the patch’s width across, against 2.6–3.0 % on an even lattice: an even lattice would need about 2,500–3,200 Gaussians for that spacing everywhere.

Outside the view the cells grow, to about 5 % of the width, but never vanish. Chapter 07 spreads one budget over whole scenes the same way.

Math

Appearance rides the same basis

Colour (spherical‑harmonic coefficients) and opacity live on the control points too — control features — and are interpolated by the same basis functions as position.

Position, scale, rotation, opacity, and 48 spherical‑harmonic coefficients for view‑dependent colour. Every Gaussian carries its own copy, and every one is optimised.

05
Adaptive refinement

Two knobs, not one

3DGS adds detail one way: clone or split a Gaussian. We have two layers: separate knobs, which have to be kept in balance.

≈ 5 min
Intuition

Start coarse

The terrain of chapter 03, now seen from above as a map. A 5 × 5 control net gets the rolling ground roughly right and misses the crater: 6.3 % error overall, 45 % at worst. Where is no mystery — error is measured per knot span, along on top, along on the right.

Below the map is the same surface cut along the dashed line and seen from the side: grey is the terrain, blue the fit, and the red between them is the error. The 5 × 5 net sails straight over the crater.

Mechanism

Layer A — insert a knot

Add a knot in the worst span and a new row of control points appears there. At the instant of insertion the surface does not change — it only gains freedom. Then the optimiser spends it: one knot is a small step, 6.3 → 6.0 %. In the side view the blue curve stays where it was when the knot lands; only a new orange control point appears.

Compare with 3DGS densification, which decides by accumulated view‑space gradients and spawns new, independent primitives.

Math

Why the surface stays put

Boehm’s knot insertion replaces the control points near the new knot by convex combinations of the old ones. Same curve, one more handle.

Intuition

Repeat where it hurts

Eighteen insertions later: 195 control points, 0.9 % error. Knots crowd over the crater. Every new parameter is a control point on the surface, so refinement adds capacity on the surface, never off it.

In a tensor product a knot adds a whole row, so the flat ground pays a little too. T‑splines would avoid that.

Mechanism

Same parameters, spent better

The adaptive net, after dropping three knots it no longer needs: 156 control points, 0.92 % error. A uniform net of the same size: 4.4 %. The best uniform net we could find near that size still sits at 3.1 %. Under each map is the same cut from the side: there the uniform net misses the terrain by 34 % of the relief, the adaptive one by 2.5 %.

All three are fitted live by the same optimiser against the same known, noise‑free height field: a demonstration of the mechanism, not a benchmark.

Intuition

Layer B — sampling density

Now change how many Gaussians are drawn. The surface does not move: 156 parameters, 0.92 % error. What moves is the drawn error, measured from the Gaussians themselves: 3.9 % with 64 of them, 0.95 % with 4,096, and from there it sits at the surface’s own 0.92 %.

Capacity lives in the knots. Rendering resolution lives in the samples. Two separate decisions.

Intuition

Balance the two, or waste one

Neither knob works alone. 25 control points drawn with 65,536 Gaussians: 6.3 % error, the same as with 1,024. 4,092 control points drawn with 64 Gaussians: 3.7 %, although the surface itself is within 0.006 % of the terrain. 400 control points with 16,384 Gaussians: 0.09 %.

Every cell of the map is measured in the page: that many Gaussians are rendered from that net and compared with the terrain. Along the green line the surface’s error and the sampling error are equal.

Two views make a point

Find the same feature in two photographs and the two rays meet in 3D. As cameras are added, points fill in: 21,760 of them. At the median a point is observed four times and lands 0.46 px from where it was found.

This is the COLMAP model that comes with the copy of the dataset used here, triangulated with the dataset’s calibrated poses. A full structure-from-motion run would also have to estimate the poses; this one did not.

Intuition

3DGS starts from round blobs

The standard start: one Gaussian per point, round, its σ from its three nearest neighbours, the point’s colour, 10 % opacity. 21,760 Gaussians, 59 numbers each, colour included. Nothing is trained yet: this fog is iteration zero.

Made here exactly as the reference implementation makes it, and drawn the way 3DGS draws: sorted and blended. Where points are sparse the blobs are wide: the fog is the 536 Gaussians wider than 1 cm, 2.5 % of them, most on the table, under it or in the background.

Switch off “σ above 1 cm” and the fog lifts
Mechanism

We start from one chart

Same points, another start: one cubic spline chart fitted through them by robust least squares. 12 × 16 control points, and every Gaussian is derived from the chart.

It lies 1.36 mm rms from the 16,799 points it kept, and 2.64 mm from the dense measured surface, which shaped none of its control points.

Its axis and angular range are borrowed from the depth-map fit of chapter 07. Fitted to the dense points themselves, a net this coarse reaches 2.00 mm: most of the gap is the net’s resolution, not the sparse data.

Mechanism

Coarse to fine: finer nets, closer

The owl of chapter 07 was refined level by level: 36 × 49 nodes, then 72 × 98, then 144 × 196, pulled onto an envelope first and then onto the measured points. The rms falls from 0.85 mm to 0.15 mm; the shipped net sits at 0.21 mm rms, 0.012 mm median.

A geometric fit to the dataset’s depth maps, not training: each level is a grid of points projected onto the measured surface. Level 1 is a little further off than the layout, and level 4 (numbers only) rises again to 0.22 mm: both shown as measured.

Intuition

Then 3DGS trains until it converges

From iteration zero, an optimiser runs for tens of thousands of steps, moving, growing, splitting and pruning Gaussians until the renders match the photographs.

That run needs a GPU and has not been made yet. On one side, its starting point; on the other, a stand-in: our chart’s coarse-to-fine levels.

Once the run is made and exported, this step replays it: the Gaussians, loss and PSNR at each saved iteration. Only then, the finished owl.

A real object: scan 122 of the DTU dataset. The charts were fitted offline to the dataset’s depth maps and photographs, 0.21 mm rms from the measured points they cover. One scene, five readings

The same Gaussians, split across the screen: appearance, depth, normals, the Gaussians themselves, and the chart each one belongs to. The bands roll, so every part of the scene passes through every reading.

A normal here is not estimated from neighbouring splats. It is , read off the surface; depth is simply where the surface is.

Drawn live from the same fitted charts — these are not the outputs of a trained model.

Intuition

An atlas of charts

Each coherent piece of surface gets its own (u, v) domain. Here there are two: the owl and its books are one sheet wrapped around the front and both sides, the table is the other. A scene with more parts takes more charts; together they are an atlas of patches that this talk loosely calls a spline manifold.

Inside a chart the surface is C². Across a seam between two charts, nothing is guaranteed here.

Mechanism

Each chart carries its own net

Shape and appearance both live on the orange points. The owl’s net is 288 × 392, a control point every 0.8 mm or so, because every feather is relief. The table is flat: its 104 × 80 points are spent on colour alone. Parameters are paid where there is something to describe.

The nets are too dense to draw whole: every ninth line of the owl’s is shown, every third of the table’s.

Intuition

A fixed budget follows the camera

The budget never changes. As the camera moves, samples flow across the (u, v) domains toward what is in view and facing the lens; a small floor keeps everything else covered. (This demo does not test occlusion.)

Watch the atlas, bottom left: brighter tiles are holding more Gaussians. The table is described more sparsely than the owl, so it gets a smaller share: there is no point sampling finer than a surface’s own detail.

Intuition

Level of detail, without retraining

The same budget, two ways. Off: spread by surface area, and the close‑up is built from coarse scales. On: spent where the camera looks.

Nothing was optimised between the two. Only the sampling changed.

Mechanism

A mesh from a list has to search

First the same Gaussians the way 3DGS stores them: an unordered list. To mesh it, every Gaussian has to find its nearest neighbours, fit a plane through them and triangulate there; a triangle stays where its corners agree.

The board counts the cost: some 70 distances per Gaussian, millions at the talk’s budget, and still bridges between parts of the surface that only lie close together, non‑manifold edges and holes.

The list is emulated from the same charts: each sample moved inside its cell, nudged off the surface, turned and resized at random, then shuffled. It was not trained. Every count and time on the board is measured in this page.

Mechanism

A mesh from the lattice is stitched

Each chart is sampled on a lattice, so every sample already knows its neighbours. A 2 × 2 window runs over it and stitches two triangles at every stop; the Gaussian at each stitched point is removed.

No search, and no bridges or holes inside a chart. The connectivity was there all along.

The lattice is as dense as the Gaussian budget: move the budget and it is stitched again. The triangles carry the samples’ own colours. The mesh downloads as PLY, GLB, OBJ or STL, in metres; the owl and the table stay two pieces, not sewn together.

Editable by construction

The owl breathes, blinks, and now and then cocks its head. Each is an edit of the net: the chest’s control points move out and back, everything above the shoulders tilts about one axis, a lid colour closes over the eyes. Thousands of Gaussians follow, still tiling, still on the surface.

Scripted motion, to show what an edit is; nothing here is learned or simulated. Each patch also exports directly as a spline surface or a mesh; sewing patches into one watertight solid is a separate step.

Intuition

Same owl, new material

Colour, opacity and shape all live on the same control points, so a new material is an edit of those and nothing else.

Clear glass: almost nothing face‑on, a mirror at the rim. Snowy owl: readers of Harry Potter will know her as Hedwig. Alien jelly: green, see‑through, and it wobbles. Each has its own properties to turn, such as opacity, gloss, stiffness or the refractive index.

It goes on the whole object, the owl and its books, on the owl alone, or on a selection you make on the atlas of control points, by colour, height, normals or DINOv3 features.

Each material is a rule over the photograph’s colours and the surface normal: for the glass, opacity and brightness follow how much light a surface of that refractive index reflects at each angle (Schlick’s approximation of Fresnel); for the snowy owl, white where the stone is bright and dark bars in the carving’s grooves; for the jelly, a third opaque where you look straight into it and more towards the rim, so what lies behind it, and its own far side, show through. Highlights are painted on and do not follow the camera. Nothing is drawn by hand or generated. The owl was told apart from its books offline, per control point, from DINOv3 features of the scan’s photographs (a pretrained model) and the crease where the owl rests on the book; a selection gets the same soft edge.

What structure buys

The surface is also an index

A 3DGS scene is a list of Gaussians in no particular order. Ours hang on spline charts, so each one has an address. That decides how hard the everyday operations are.

≈ 3 min
Intuition

The same Gaussians, stored two ways

Colour each Gaussian by its position in memory. In a list, neighbours in memory are strangers in space. On charts, memory order is surface order: chart by chart, row by row.

The list is emulated: the same surfaces sampled at jittered places, nudged off the surface, turned, resized and shuffled. It is not a trained 3DGS model.

Mechanism

Neighbours: search, or read

To find what lies next to a Gaussian, a list needs a spatial index, built first and rebuilt whenever Gaussians move. And distance alone cannot tell the books from the table they stand on: the red neighbours belong to another surface.

On a chart the neighbours are the next addresses, . Nothing is searched, and they are neighbours along the surface.

Mechanism

A mesh, for the asking

Join each address to the next and there are the triangles, at whatever resolution was sampled: the lattice is the tessellation. Joining a list’s nearest neighbours gives a scribble, with red edges bridging separate surfaces.

Real pipelines do not stop at that scribble. They extract a mesh from a list with TSDF fusion or Poisson reconstruction: a separate algorithm with its own errors, which is not run here.

Mechanism

Clustering the list by position cuts the table in two and hands one half to the owl. On charts the part is the chart, and any finer cut is a 2D image operation in (u, v).

A list against charts · contact

Can’t touch this

A game, a physics engine and an editor all begin with the same question: where is the surface, and which way does it face? A list of Gaussians has no answer. A spline does.

≈ 2½ min

♪ Original loop · synthesized in the browser as the page loads

Intuition

Drop a ball on a list: nothing to hit

A list of Gaussians is a density, not a boundary. With no rule, the ball falls straight through. Treat every Gaussian as a loose particle and the ball knocks them away, because nothing holds them together.

A real pipeline first picks a density threshold or extracts a mesh: a separate algorithm with its own errors. The list is emulated from the same bowl; it is not a trained 3DGS model.

Mechanism

When does it touch? A list gives seven answers

Slow the last centimetres down and measure every way a list can say “contact”: the ball reaches the 3, 2 or 1 sigma shell of the nearest Gaussian, the summed density at its lowest point crosses 0.1, 0.3 or 0.5, it reaches the nearest centre. Here the seven answers spread over 24 ms and 6 cm of the fall.

The spline gives one: the root of , with the closest point, found to a residual at rounding level.

Only the camera and the slow motion are scripted (time runs 20× slower from 10 cm above the surface). Each answer clicks; the spline’s is the knock. The spread is this emulated list’s, not a measured property of trained 3DGS models.

Press drop again
Mechanism

On a spline, contact is a formula

Find the closest point to the ball, take the normal , reflect the velocity about it. Restitution is one material constant: set it to 1 and the energy stays at 100 %, bounce after bounce.

The closest point takes five Gauss–Newton steps in (u, v), started from the previous answer. The ball is a point mass with a radius; it does not spin.

Mechanism

Put the control points on springs

The contact force goes to the sixteen control points that shape the surface at the contact point (u, v), shared out by their basis weights. The surface gives, throws the ball back, and the ripple runs through the net. The Gaussians are derived again every frame.

Intuition

Out of bounds

A hard bounce throws the ball out of the spline bowl and into the list. There every Gaussian is on its own, and the bowl comes apart.

Only the direction of the launch is chosen, so that the ball lands in the other bowl. The bounce, the flight and the scatter are simulated. How hard overlapping Gaussians push each other apart is an invented constant: a list defines no such force.

Takeaway

The surface is not reconstructed. It is the representation.

StructuredEvery Gaussian is centred on a surface and cannot leave it. Whether that surface is right is what the optimiser still has to earn.
ContinuousProperties vary smoothly over the parametric domain — a built‑in regulariser.
Resolution‑adaptiveChange the sample density at any time, without retraining. Detail itself is capped by the control net.
CompactLow‑order colour on a control net. The paper projects 10–50× fewer optimised parameters; equal‑quality measurements are pending.
InteroperableThe model is a set of spline patches: each exports as a surface or a mesh. Watertight stitching is future work.
